When you press Play with two editor instances on the same machine, the "network" is a memory copy. Packets arrive
in microseconds, in order, every time. Then a friend in another country joins, and the same code shows enemies
teleporting, doors opening late and shots that clearly hit but don't count. Nothing in your code changed. The **link**
changed.

Every link between two machines can be described with four numbers: <Term id="latency">latency</Term>,
<Term id="jitter">jitter</Term>, <Term id="packet-loss">packet loss</Term> and <Term id="bandwidth">bandwidth</Term>.
The rest of this course is a toolbox for hiding those four numbers from the player. Before you can hide them, you need
to know how big they really are.

## Latency: where the milliseconds come from

Latency is the time it takes a packet to get from one machine to the other. Games almost always talk about the
<Term id="rtt">round-trip time (RTT)</Term> instead: the time from sending a message to receiving the reply. The
"<Term id="ping">ping</Term>" figure on a scoreboard is usually RTT in milliseconds. If a path is symmetric, one-way
latency is roughly RTT / 2, but real paths are often asymmetric, so treat that as an estimate.

A packet's latency is the sum of four delays:

| Delay | What causes it | Typical size |
|---|---|---|
| **Propagation** | Light in optical fibre travels at about 200,000 km/s, roughly 5 ms per 1000 km | 0–150 ms one way |
| **Serialization** | Pushing the packet's bits onto the wire: size ÷ link speed | 1200 bytes at 1 Mbit/s ≈ 9.6 ms; at 100 Mbit/s ≈ 0.1 ms |
| **Queuing** | Waiting in router buffers behind other traffic | 0 ms on an idle link, hundreds of ms on a saturated one |
| **Processing** | Routers, Wi-Fi, the OS network stack, your own game loop | Microseconds per router, but several ms for Wi-Fi |

Propagation is the one you can't optimise away. London to New York is about 5,600 km, so even a perfectly straight fibre
gives 28 ms one way and **56 ms RTT**. Real cables don't follow great circles, so measured RTT is closer to 70–80 ms.
Sydney to Frankfurt is around 16,500 km, which is a physical minimum of 165 ms RTT and 250–300 ms in practice.

Typical RTTs you should design for:

| Connection | Typical RTT |
|---|---|
| Wired LAN | Under 1 ms |
| Same city or region, wired broadband | 5–30 ms |
| Across a continent | 40–90 ms |
| Transatlantic | 70–120 ms |
| Europe ↔ Australia | 250–320 ms |
| Mobile 4G | 40–100 ms, with frequent spikes |

### How much latency can a game take?

The answer depends on the genre and on which techniques you use to hide it. Rough budgets, assuming good netcode:

- **Fighting games and rhythm games**: every 16.7 ms frame counts. These games use rollback (lesson 12) and still feel worse beyond about 100–150 ms RTT.
- **Competitive shooters**: under 50 ms feels great, 100 ms is playable, and beyond 150 ms players notice corrections and "shot behind cover" deaths.
- **Co-op action and MMOs**: 100–200 ms is fine with prediction and interpolation.
- **Strategy and turn-based games**: 250 ms or more is acceptable, because commands are naturally scheduled ahead.

Remember that the network is only part of what the player feels. Input sampling, your frame time, the render queue
and the display add 30–100 ms of **input-to-photon** latency even offline. Network latency is added on top.

## Jitter: latency that won't sit still

If every packet took exactly 50 ms, games would be easy: you'd delay everything by 50 ms and play it back smoothly.
In reality, consecutive packets take 48 ms, 51 ms, 73 ms, 49 ms... That **variation** is jitter. It comes mostly from
queuing: your packet happened to arrive at a router just behind someone's video stream, or the Wi-Fi radio had to
retry a frame.

Jitter matters because game data is **periodic**. A server sending 30 snapshots per second sends one every 33 ms. With
20 ms of jitter, the client sees gaps of 13 ms, then 53 ms, then two snapshots in the same frame. If you render each
snapshot when it arrives, motion stutters even though nothing was lost.

The standard cure is a <Term id="jitter-buffer">jitter buffer</Term>: hold incoming data for a short, fixed delay and
then play it out at an even pace. You're trading a little latency for smoothness. You'll build exactly this for remote
players in the interpolation lesson.

## Packet loss: some packets never arrive

Packet loss is the fraction of packets that never reach their destination. Most loss is **congestion**: a router
queue is full, so new packets are dropped. Wi-Fi interference, mobile handovers between cell towers and faulty cables
add more. Typical numbers:

- Wired broadband: 0–0.5%.
- Good Wi-Fi: 0.5–2%.
- Bad Wi-Fi or mobile: 2–10%, often in **bursts**, where several packets in a row disappear while the radio struggles.

Bursts matter more than averages. A 2% average spread evenly is barely noticeable at 30 updates per second, but the
same 2% arriving as one 300 ms blackout every 15 seconds is a visible freeze. When you test, simulate bursty loss as
well as random loss.

Loss interacts with your delivery choices from lesson 1. For unreliable state, a lost update is simply replaced by the
next one. For reliable messages, a loss costs at least one extra round trip while the sender notices and resends,
which is why reliable events "feel" laggier than state on a bad connection. A common trick is **redundancy**: each
packet repeats the last few inputs, so one lost packet costs nothing.

## Bandwidth and throughput

**Bandwidth** is a link's capacity, such as "100 Mbit/s down, 10 Mbit/s up". <Term id="throughput">Throughput</Term> is
what you actually achieve, which is lower because of headers, retransmissions, other traffic and congestion.

Three facts matter to game developers:

1. **Upload is usually the bottleneck.** Home connections are often asymmetric, with 5–20× less upload than download. If a
   player hosts a game, their *upload* has to carry every other player's updates.
2. **Bits, not bytes.** Link speeds are quoted in bits per second, and packet sizes in bytes. 1 KB/s = 8 kbit/s.
3. **Saturation creates latency.** If you send more than the link can carry, packets queue in the router, and that queue
   can add hundreds of milliseconds of delay before anything is dropped (this is called <Term id="bufferbloat">bufferbloat</Term>). A game that
   floods its link makes its **own** latency worse.

A useful budget: keep a client's steady-state traffic under about 64–256 kbit/s (8–32 KB/s) each way for most action
games. A server's total upload is that number multiplied by the player count.

## Packet size and the MTU

Every link has a <Term id="mtu">maximum transmission unit (MTU)</Term>, the largest packet it carries in one piece.
Ethernet's is 1500 bytes. Some of that goes to headers: 20 bytes for IPv4 (40 for IPv6) plus 8 bytes for UDP. And the
smallest MTU anywhere on the path wins: PPPoE DSL lines use 1492, VPNs and tunnels lose another 50–100 bytes, and IPv6
only guarantees 1280 bytes end to end.

If a packet is bigger than the path MTU, IP <Term id="fragmentation">fragmentation</Term> splits it, or a router drops it.
Fragments are reassembled only if **every** fragment arrives, so fragmentation multiplies loss:

> P(packet arrives) = (1 − loss)^fragments

With 5% loss, a 4-fragment packet arrives only 0.95⁴ ≈ 81% of the time, so it is effectively lost **19%** of the time.

That is why most netcode libraries keep each UDP payload at or below about **1200 bytes**: comfortably under the
1280-byte IPv6 minimum after headers, and safe on almost every path. Netcode that needs to send something larger
splits it into its own pieces and resends just the missing ones.

What you should notice:

- On **LAN**, everything arrives instantly and in order. This is why "it works on my machine" means nothing for netcode.
- On **Mobile 4G**, the average RTT is acceptable, but individual packets vary widely. Spacing on arrival is uneven even though sending was perfectly regular.
- On **Bad Wi-Fi**, losses come in **clusters**, and jitter spikes are bigger than the average latency.
- On **Intercontinental**, there's very little jitter or loss, but the base latency is high and nothing you do in software can lower it. You can only hide it.

## Measuring the link in code

Netcode libraries measure RTT continuously by timestamping packets and timing the reply. They then smooth the samples,
because a single spike shouldn't change gameplay decisions. The smoothing below is the one TCP uses (RFC 6298), and
the jitter estimate is the one from RTP (RFC 3550). Both are simple exponential moving averages.

```csharp title="LinkStats.cs"
// Engine-agnostic: call OnPong when the reply to a timestamped ping arrives.
public sealed class LinkStats
{
    public double SmoothedRttMs { get; private set; } = -1; // -1 = no sample yet
    public double RttVariationMs { get; private set; }
    public double JitterMs { get; private set; }

    double _lastTransitMs = double.NaN;

    public void OnPong(double sentAtMs, double nowMs)
    {
        double sample = nowMs - sentAtMs;
        if (SmoothedRttMs < 0)
        {
            SmoothedRttMs = sample;
            RttVariationMs = sample / 2;
            return;
        }
        // RFC 6298: alpha = 1/8, beta = 1/4.
        RttVariationMs += 0.25 * (System.Math.Abs(SmoothedRttMs - sample) - RttVariationMs);
        SmoothedRttMs += 0.125 * (sample - SmoothedRttMs);
    }

    // RFC 3550 interarrival jitter: call for each periodic packet with sender and receiver clocks.
    // Clock offset cancels out because only the *difference* in transit time matters.
    public void OnPeriodicPacket(double senderTimeMs, double arrivalMs)
    {
        double transit = arrivalMs - senderTimeMs;
        if (!double.IsNaN(_lastTransitMs))
            JitterMs += (System.Math.Abs(transit - _lastTransitMs) - JitterMs) / 16.0;
        _lastTransitMs = transit;
    }
}
```

You'll rarely write this yourself, but you'll read these numbers constantly. Netcode for GameObjects exposes the
transport's RTT per client through the `NetworkTransport` method `GetCurrentRtt(clientId)`, and Steam's connection status reports
ping and quality per connection.

## Pitfalls

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Testing only on localhost">
  On localhost, RTT is 0, jitter is 0 and loss is 0, so every timing bug stays hidden. Bugs like "the door opens before the
  animation" or "the RPC arrives before the object spawns" only appear with latency. Develop with at least 100 ms RTT, 20 ms
  jitter and 2% loss simulated.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Looking at averages only">
  "Average ping 40 ms" can hide a 95th percentile of 180 ms. Players remember the spikes. When you log network stats, record
  percentiles (p95, p99) and the longest gap between received packets, not just the mean.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Big packets because 'the MTU is 1500'">
  The path MTU is the *smallest* MTU of every link on the way, and you can't see it. A 1450-byte payload that works at home
  can be silently dropped on a player's VPN. Keep payloads at about 1200 bytes or less, and let your netcode library fragment
  anything larger.
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Latency** is propagation + serialization + queuing + processing. Propagation is about 5 ms per 1000 km of fibre and can't be optimised away.
- Games reason in **RTT**. Budgets range from about 50 ms for competitive shooters to 250 ms or more for strategy games.
- **Jitter** is variation in latency. A small, fixed buffer turns uneven arrival into smooth playback, at the cost of a little extra delay.
- **Packet loss** is mostly congestion, often bursty. Unreliable state shrugs it off, while reliable messages pay at least an extra round trip.
- **Bandwidth** is capacity and **throughput** is what you get. Upload is the usual bottleneck, and saturating a link adds queuing latency.
- Keep UDP payloads at or below about **1200 bytes**: fragmentation multiplies loss, and the path MTU can be smaller than you expect.

</KeyTakeaways>
